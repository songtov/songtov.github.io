Part 2 of the Startup series. [Part 1](/posts/startup-solo-saas-mindset/) was about the mindset: many cheap bets instead of one big one.

If you're going to make many bets, you need a steady supply of ideas. The good news is that you don't need brilliant ones. You need small, specific ones you can ship quickly.

---

## Stop Looking for an Original Idea

Most first-time founders wait for an idea nobody has had before. That's backwards.

If nobody has built it, that's often because nobody wants it. If other products already exist and people pay for them, that's proof of demand. As a solo founder, you don't need to invent a market. You need a slightly better, cheaper, simpler, or more focused version for a group of people you understand.

Specific beats clever. "Invoices for Stripe customers" is a better solo-SaaS idea than "a platform to reinvent finance."

---

## Three Places to Look

**1. Your own friction.** What do you keep doing by hand? What tool do you wish existed? You understand the problem deeply, and you're your own first tester.

**2. Markets where people already pay.** Look at categories with clear spending: analytics, invoicing, email, scheduling, payments. If big companies sell it, there's room for a small, focused version.

**3. Audiences you can reach.** An idea is only useful if you can get it in front of buyers. If you already hang out with developers, designers, or small business owners, build for them.

The sweet spot is where all three overlap.

## What Marc Lou's Product List Shows

Look at the products listed on Marc Lou's site and a pattern jumps out. Almost all of them are tools for people like him: indie founders shipping small online products.

- **ShipFast**: a boilerplate to "ship your startup in days," the exact problem he had launching product after product.
- **DataFast**: web analytics for founders.
- **ByeDispute** and **Zenvoice**: tools for preventing Stripe payment disputes and handling invoices, the kind of chores every small SaaS deals with.
- **TrustMRR**: a database of verified startup revenue, for an audience that loves revenue screenshots.

None of these categories is new. Analytics, invoicing, and boilerplates all existed before. What he added was focus on one audience, simple products, and the ability to reach that audience directly through his following.

That's the lesson: **he didn't need original ideas. He needed ideas his audience would recognize instantly.**

---

## A 30-Second Filter

Before building anything, ask three questions:

1. **Can I ship a first version in days?** If it needs months, it's too big for now. Cut it down or skip it.
2. **Does anyone already pay for something like this?** Existing competitors are a good sign, not a bad one.
3. **Do I know where the buyers hang out?** If you can't name the community, newsletter, or channel, you'll struggle to launch.

Three yeses: build it. Any no: put it back on the list.

---

## Keep an Idea List, Then Pick Fast

Ideas come while you work, not while you brainstorm. Keep a running list: annoyances, tools you paid for and disliked, things people complain about in your communities.

When it's time to start something, pick from the list quickly. Don't spend weeks choosing. The next part of this series is about validating an idea, and you'll learn far more from a small test than from more thinking.

---

## Takeaways

- Original ideas aren't the goal; proven demand is.
- Look where your own problem, an existing market, and reachable buyers overlap.
- Marc Lou's products mostly serve one audience he knows well: indie founders.
- If it can't ship in days, make it smaller.
