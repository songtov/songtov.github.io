Part 3 of the Startup series. [Part 2](/posts/startup-finding-ideas/) was about finding small, specific ideas where your own problem, an existing market, and reachable buyers overlap.

Now you have an idea on the list. Before spending weeks on it, you want some evidence that someone cares. The trick is getting that evidence cheaply.

---

## Opinions Are Free

The most common validation mistake is asking people, "Would you use this?"

Almost everyone says yes. It's polite, it costs them nothing, and they're imagining a perfect version of your product. A hundred "sounds cool" replies tell you very little.

What you want instead is a **commitment**: something that costs the other person time, attention, or money. The more it costs them, the more it tells you.

![A four-step ladder from weak to strong signals: sounds cool or likes, email signup, reply or call, pre-order or payment](/images/startup-validating-ideas.svg)

Likes are the weakest signal. An email signup is a little better. A reply or a call means someone cares enough to spend time on you. A pre-order or payment is as close to proof as you get before the product exists.

---

## Three Cheap Tests

**1. A landing page.** One page: the problem, your promise, a price, and a button. The button collects an email or, better, takes a pre-order. You can build it in an afternoon.

**2. A post where your buyers already are.** Describe the problem in a community you're part of and see who replies. Pay attention to people who describe the same pain in their own words, or ask when they can try it.

**3. Do it by hand.** Before automating anything, deliver the result manually for a few people. If nobody wants it when it's free and personal, software won't fix that.

Each of these takes hours or days, not weeks. That's the rule: **validation should cost less than building.**

---

## When the Launch Is the Test

Here's the twist for solo SaaS. If you can build a first version in a weekend, a separate validation step may cost about as much as just shipping.

That's how Marc Lou's product list reads. His site lists 43 startups. Many show little revenue. As of this writing, LaunchViral, BioAge, and Sponsor My Body each show $0 in the last 30 days. Some were sold for small amounts: MakeLanding for $35K, GameWidget for $4K. VirallyBot reached $4K MRR, then was "rekt by COVID" and shut down.

His site doesn't describe a formal validation process. But the pattern is clear: ship small, put it in front of an audience, and let real usage and revenue decide. The products themselves are the experiments.

His Ship or Die tagline puts it bluntly: "SHIP your app in 30 days, or die." A time box like that is a validation tool too. It stops you from polishing an idea nobody has tested.

So the choice depends on your build cost:

- **Days to build**: ship it. The launch is the test.
- **Weeks or months to build**: run a cheap test first.

---

## Decide the Kill Rule First

Validation only works if you're honest about the result. The easiest way is to write down, before the test, what "no" looks like.

For example: "If fewer than 20 people sign up in two weeks, I drop it." Or: "If nobody pre-orders after I post in three communities, I move on."

Without a rule, every result looks promising. Ten signups feels like momentum when you want it to work. Setting the bar in advance keeps you from spending three more months on an idea the market already rejected.

A "no" is not a failure. It's a cheap answer that frees you for the next idea on the list.

---

## Takeaways

- Ask for commitments, not opinions.
- Emails beat likes; payments beat everything.
- Validation should cost less than building. If the MVP takes a weekend, ship it.
- Write the kill rule before you run the test.
