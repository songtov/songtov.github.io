Part 6 of the Startup series. [Part 5](/posts/startup-pricing/) was about charging from day one and picking a price model.

A price only matters if someone sees it. This part is about getting the first people to your page and the first few to pay, with no audience and no budget.

---

## The Headline Does Most of the Work

Before you post anywhere, fix the first line of your landing page.

Marc Lou is blunt about this. In his newsletter post on getting a first customer, he writes: "Spend at least 1 hour crafting your headline. It's 80% of a successful launch." In another post he adds that "80% of people don't scroll past the headline."

Those are his estimates, not measured laws. But the point holds: most visitors decide in seconds. A good headline says **who it's for** and **what they get**. "Analytics for your website" is vague. "See which marketing channels bring paying customers" is a reason to keep reading.

---

## Free Launch Channels

You don't need ads for your first customers. You need a few places where the right people are already looking.

**Launch sites.** Product Hunt, Hacker News, and Reddit are the usual starting points, and Marc Lou has written separate guides for each. Every one of them rewards a clear headline and a genuine post over a sales pitch.

**Your communities.** Forums, Discord servers, and Slack groups where your buyers already hang out. If you followed [Part 2](/posts/startup-finding-ideas/), you picked an idea partly because you know where these people are.

One launch post won't make a business. But a single good day of traffic is often enough to get the first few signups and learn what people actually click on.

---

## Cold Outreach for the First Few

For the first handful of customers, writing to people directly works better than you'd expect.

His advice on cold messages is short: keep them "5 sentences max," make them personal and about the benefit to the reader, and remember that "Quantity doesn't matter. Quality does."

Ten thoughtful messages to people who clearly have the problem beat two hundred copy-pasted ones. This works especially well for B2B tools or higher prices, where one customer is worth the effort.

---

## Launch 100 Times

Here's the part most founders miss. A quiet launch feels like a verdict. It usually isn't.

![A loop: launch, measure visits, signups, and payments, change the pitch, launch again, with an exit to first customers](/images/startup-first-customers.svg)

In "Launch your startup 100 times," Marc Lou argues that nobody keeps track of how many times you've launched, so you can launch the same product again with a different pitch. He lists three ways:

- **Rebrand**: same product, new name, domain, and hero section.
- **Reposition**: same product, copy aimed at a narrower customer.
- **Repurpose**: turn one feature into a free tool that leads to the paid product.

His own example is DataFast. He says it converted at 0.22% when it launched as an "analytics tool," and at 1.45% after he repositioned it as a "revenue attribution tool" (his self-reported numbers). Same product, different words. His summary: "Small changes in words = big changes in conversions."

---

## When Nothing Works

If you've relaunched a few times and still nobody pays, he suggests checking two things.

Either the product "doesn't solve a real problem," in which case you move on, or the landing page copy "isn't converting," in which case you fix the copy.

That's a useful split. The first means a new idea from your list. The second means another lap around the loop. Either way, he notes, persistence counts: "if you show up for long enough, luck will find you."

---

## Takeaways

- Spend real time on the headline: who it's for and what they get.
- Use free launch sites and your own communities before anything paid.
- Short, personal cold messages win the first few customers.
- A quiet launch isn't a verdict. Rebrand, reposition, or repurpose, then launch again.
