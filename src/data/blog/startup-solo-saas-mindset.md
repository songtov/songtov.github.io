Part 1 of the Startup series.

I'm more interested in building my own products than in climbing a career ladder. This series is my notes on one specific path: going from nothing to a profitable, bootstrapped SaaS as a solo founder. My main reference is Marc Lou, a French indie hacker who publishes his products and revenue openly.

The first lesson isn't about code or ideas. It's about how you think about the odds.

---

## The Solo, Bootstrapped Path

The default startup story is: raise money, hire a team, grow fast. The solo path flips almost every part of that.

- **No investors**: You don't need permission, and nobody needs you to become a unicorn.
- **No employees**: Costs stay tiny. A product with a few hundred dollars a month in revenue is already profitable.
- **Full control**: You pick what to build, when to stop, and how to live.

Marc Lou's site describes exactly this setup: working solo since 2021, zero employees, zero investors, and he claims around 90% margins. He also says he started because he got "fired everywhere." That's worth remembering. Nobody handed him the path; he made it after other options ran out.

---

## The Math of Many Small Bets

Here's the uncomfortable part: most products fail. Not some. Most.

Marc Lou's own site lists 43 startups. Many of them show little or no revenue. A few were sold for small amounts: MakeLanding for $35K, GameWidget for $4K. One SaaS, VirallyBot, reached $4K in monthly revenue before COVID killed it.

And a handful carry everything. In his self-reported numbers at the time of writing, TrustMRR and DataFast bring in most of the money, while ShipFast, the product that made him famous, now earns a small fraction of what it used to.

![A bar chart where most products are small gray bars and a few are tall colored bars](/images/startup-solo-saas-mindset.svg)

That's not a story of one genius idea. It's a portfolio. Even the winners change over time.

The lesson: **you can't reliably pick the winner in advance, so make it cheap to try many times.** If each attempt costs a few weeks and almost no money, failing ten times is survivable. If each attempt costs a year and your savings, failing twice ends the game.

---

## Speed Is the Strategy

Cheap attempts only work if they're fast. That's why "ship fast" shows up everywhere in Marc Lou's work. ShipFast itself is a boilerplate sold on the promise of shipping a startup "in days, not months."

Speed isn't about being sloppy. It's about getting to the only feedback that matters as early as possible: do real people use it, and will they pay?

A month spent polishing a product nobody wants is a month you could have spent on the next attempt.

---

## The Real Constraints

When you start, it feels like the hard part is finding an idea or writing the code. It usually isn't.

The real constraints are:

- **Distribution**: Getting anyone to see what you built. Marc Lou's audience on X, YouTube, and his newsletter is a large part of why his launches work. He built that audience by posting consistently, long before most of his products made money.
- **Consistency**: Shipping again after the fifth failure.
- **Runway**: How long you can keep trying before you need income. Low personal costs buy you more attempts.

Code is the part you control best. The rest is where most solo founders quit.

## What's Next in This Series

The upcoming parts follow the path roughly in order: finding an idea, validating it before building, shipping an MVP in days, pricing, getting first customers, building in public, SEO, tracking MRR, running several products, and deciding when to quit or double down.

One note on numbers: Marc Lou's revenue figures are **self-reported** and change month to month. I'll quote them from his own site with that caveat, and treat them as examples, not benchmarks.

---

## Takeaways

- Solo and bootstrapped means tiny costs, which makes small wins profitable.
- Most products fail; plan for a portfolio, not a single bet.
- Make each attempt cheap and fast so you can afford many.
- Distribution, consistency, and runway matter more than the idea.
