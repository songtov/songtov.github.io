Part 5 of the Startup series. [Part 4](/posts/startup-shipping-mvp/) was about shipping an MVP in days: one paid job, plus a way to pay.

That second half matters more than it sounds. This part is about what to charge, and how.

---

## Free Is Expensive

Making your product free feels like a safe way to get users. For a solo founder, it's usually the opposite.

Free users still need support. They still find bugs and request features. But they tell you nothing about the one question that matters: **will anyone pay?** And they don't add a single day of runway.

Charging from day one keeps the experiment honest. Ten paying users tell you more than a thousand free signups.

---

## One-Time or Subscription?

Pick the model that matches how the product is used.

![Two panels: pay once fits boilerplates, templates, and courses, with cash up front but every month starting at zero; subscription fits analytics, monitoring, and hosting, with a slower start but revenue that compounds](/images/startup-pricing.svg)

If the buyer **owns a thing** after buying, like code, a template, or a course, a one-time price is natural. Nobody wants a monthly bill for a file they downloaded.

If the buyer **keeps getting value** every month, like data, uptime, or automation, a subscription is fair. You're paying for servers every month too.

Marc Lou's two best-known products show both. As of November 2026:

- **ShipFast** is a one-time purchase: "Pay once. Build unlimited projects!" The Starter plan is $199 and All-in is $249, shown as discounted from $299 and $349, with "Lifetime updates."
- **DataFast** is a subscription: Starter at $9/month and Growth at $19/month.

A boilerplate is something you own. Analytics only makes sense if it keeps running. The pricing follows the product.

Both models have a cost. One-time sales bring cash up front, but every month starts at zero, and you need a steady flow of new buyers. Subscriptions start slower, but revenue builds month on month, and that recurring number (MRR) is what makes a SaaS valuable.

---

## Price Higher Than Feels Comfortable

Almost every first-time founder underprices. It feels safer, but it isn't.

A low price means you need many more customers to reach the same revenue. More customers means more support, which as a solo founder means less time to build. Cheap customers also tend to be the most demanding.

A useful exercise: pick a revenue goal and divide. If you want $3,000 a month, that's 1,000 customers at $3 or 100 customers at $30. The second is far easier to find and serve.

You can always lower a price later. Raising it on existing customers is much harder.

---

## Keep the Tiers Simple

Two or three plans is plenty. More than that and buyers stall trying to compare.

Make the difference between plans easy to see, usually one limit like the number of projects, sites, or seats. Then make one plan the obvious default. Both ShipFast and DataFast show only a few plans with clear differences.

---

## Trials Beat Free Plans

If people need to try the product before paying, use a **time-limited trial**, not a permanent free plan.

DataFast offers a 14-day free trial with "No card required" and no free plan. A trial still ends in the same honest question, "will you pay?", just two weeks later. A free plan lets people avoid that question forever.

ShipFast takes a stricter line: since you get the code immediately, its FAQ says it can't be refunded once you have repo access. If the product can't be "returned," a trial or refund may not make sense at all.

---

## Takeaways

- Charge from day one. Free users don't prove demand or pay for runway.
- One-time for things people own; subscriptions for ongoing value.
- Price higher than feels comfortable. Fewer, better customers win.
- Two or three plans, one obvious choice, and a trial instead of a free plan.
